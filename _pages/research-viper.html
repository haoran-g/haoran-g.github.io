VIPER
VIPER: A System-Level Performance Model for Exploring Processing-in-Memory Designs
Venue: Under Review — ACM International Conference on Architectural Support for Programming Languages and Operating Systems (ASPLOS), 2027
Authors: Haoran Geng et al.
Background

Processing-in-Memory (PIM) architectures promise to reduce data movement overhead by computing in or near memory, but evaluating whether a PIM design actually delivers system-level benefit is extremely difficult. Circuit- and array-level simulators miss full-system effects. Cycle-accurate gem5 simulations require 6–30 hours per configuration and significant engineering effort to extend. No existing tool covers both PNM (Processing Near Memory) and PUM (Processing Using Memory) paradigms under a unified, fast framework, leaving PIM designers without an efficient path to design-space exploration (DSE). Key cross-layer costs — data transfer overhead, NVM programming latency, capacity constraints, and non-offloadable host work — are invisible at the device level but fundamentally determine end-to-end performance.

Design

VIPER builds on the classical Average Memory Access Time (AMAT) model, extending it with PIM-specific terms. For task-offloading, total execution time separates into CPU and PIM terms: PIM cost includes data transfer (t_in, t_out), array-to-PE movement (t_array for PNM), or NVM programming latency (t_prog for PUM), plus capacity partitioning overhead (⌈D_PIM/C_PIM⌉ multiplier). For data-triggered execution, t_DRAM in the AMAT equation is replaced by t_PIM,mem. CPU-side parameters are fixed by a single Linux perf profiling pass; PIM-side parameters are swept for DSE. VIPER completes a full sweep in under one minute.

Validated against 400+ gem5-SALAM cycle-accurate simulation runs across diverse PIM configurations, benchmarks, and CPU types. VIPER achieves below 10% mean estimation error (97% of task-offloading points within ±10% envelope; Pearson r=0.995 for data-triggering). Case studies on UPMEM and IMCRYPTO reveal that fast in-cell computation alone does not guarantee speedup — transfer/dispatch overhead and capacity constraints dominate in practice.

Conclusion

VIPER provides PIM designers with a fast, accurate system-level tool that reveals cross-layer costs invisible at the device level — programming latency, capacity walls, and Amdahl ceilings — before committing to silicon. It is open-sourced to serve the broader PIM research community.
